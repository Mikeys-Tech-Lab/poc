The usual AI productivity question is:

**How much faster can work become?**

That question starts too late.

Before asking how much output increases, we need to ask what happens to the
capacity created by the increase.

A productivity gain can become:

- shorter work
- more care
- safer staffing
- deeper learning
- better documentation
- stronger accessibility
- higher wages
- more public value

It can also become:

- layoffs
- speedup
- deskilling
- surveillance
- higher quotas
- hidden verification work
- weaker entry-level pathways
- greater returns to owners

The technology does not choose between these outcomes.

Institutions do.

But an institution is not one actor. Owners, executives, managers, workers,
unions, customers, creditors, public funders, regulators, and procurement
systems can pull in different directions. A manager can transmit pressure they
did not create. A small employer or public service can be constrained by debt,
budgets, competition, procurement rules, or staffing shortages while still
holding more immediate decision power over workers.

Shared constraint does not imply equal power. Structural clarity asks where the
pressure came from, who can refuse it, who can transfer its cost, who can capture
the gain, and who bears the hardest consequences.

## Productivity measures a change, not a moral result

Productivity compares inputs and outputs. It can help identify waste, friction,
or useful support.

But the metric says nothing by itself about distribution.

An organization can report improved productivity while workers lose autonomy,
quality declines, or risk is moved onto people with less power to challenge it.

The ILO's refined 2025 exposure index estimates that one in four workers
worldwide is in an occupation with some generative AI exposure. Only 3.3 percent
of global employment falls into the highest exposure category. Clerical work
remains the most exposed, and exposure is uneven by gender and national income.

The report's governing conclusion is transformation rather than automatic
replacement.<SourceHook sourceId="global-occupational-exposure" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

The boundary matters:

- exposure is not displacement
- task capability is not a staffing decision
- transformation is not automatically progress
- technical possibility does not determine institutional use

A 2026 ILO review adds a second boundary. Productivity gains are real but uneven
and sometimes unverified. Large-scale job displacement remains limited in the
available evidence. Worker-reported time savings have often not translated into
higher measured output, earnings, or employment. The effects appear more clearly
in task organization, worker autonomy, opportunity, and job quality.<SourceHook sourceId="current-empirical-synthesis" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

This is exactly why the distribution question cannot wait for a final prediction
about the future.

## The evidence is real and contradictory

Several findings can be true at the same time.

A field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers found that users
of an integrated generative AI tool spent about two fewer hours on email per week
and worked less outside regular hours. It did not detect wider changes in task
quantity or composition during the experiment.<SourceHook sourceId="measured-time-relief" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

A study of 5,172 customer-support agents found a 15 percent average increase in
issues resolved per hour, with larger gains for less experienced and lower-skill
workers. It found some durable learning and improved work experience, while also
finding a small decline in conversation quality among the most skilled workers
when they followed AI suggestions. It could not observe wages, total labor
demand, or economy-wide hiring effects.<SourceHook sourceId="measured-workplace-productivity" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

A Danish study found rapid adoption, reported productivity benefits, and
widespread creation of new AI-related tasks. It found no measurable average
effect on earnings or recorded hours in the first two years, while work was
reorganized around content generation, oversight, and integration.<SourceHook sourceId="task-reorganization" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

These studies do not cancel one another.

Together they show:

- some workers save real time
- some workflows produce substantial gains
- new tasks appear around the technology
- aggregate wage and hour effects may remain small
- organizational use determines what the gain becomes
- the evidence does not support one universal workplace story

Contradiction is not confusion here.

It is the structure.

## The conversion mechanism: relief becomes a target

Many tools are introduced through the language of relief:

less administration, less repetition, less friction.

But the gain can pass through a familiar chain:

1. A task becomes faster.
2. The saved time is described as capacity.
3. Capacity becomes a target.
4. The target becomes a metric.
5. The metric becomes discipline.

The worker experiences higher expectations.

The dashboard still reports success.

This is the difference between **speed** and **relief**.

Relief returns capacity to people.

Speedup captures capacity for the institution.

## The verification tax

AI outputs can arrive before the ground needed to trust them.

A generated summary may reduce first-pass reading time while creating new work
around omissions and framing.

A generated policy may reduce drafting time while requiring legal, social, and
factual review.

Generated code may reduce implementation time while increasing architectural,
security, and maintenance review.

A welfare recommendation, hiring score, or clinical suggestion may reduce
administrative time while increasing the need for explanation, appeal, and harm
prevention.

This creates what I call the verification tax:

- prompting
- checking
- correction
- documentation
- privacy review
- explanation
- appeal
- repair
- shared learning

The term is an analytical proposal, not an established economic measure.

The tax becomes extraction when institutions count the generation gain but treat
the grounding work as invisible.

An honest productivity measure must include the whole workflow, including
failure and repair.

## Raising the floor, cutting the ladder

AI can spread patterns that previously depended on access to experienced
colleagues. That can widen access and reduce arbitrary gatekeeping.

But organizations can also extract tacit knowledge, standardize it, and reduce
investment in junior roles.

This produces a structural tension:

- AI can make entry into skilled work easier.
- Cost-cutting can remove the work through which skill develops.

The 2026 ILO evidence review identifies weaker employment opportunities for
younger workers as an emerging risk, while warning that the evidence is still
early.

A workplace cannot indefinitely weaken its learning pipeline while continuing
to demand mature judgment.

Junior work is not merely cheap output. It is part of the infrastructure through
which future expertise is created.

This is why productivity analysis must include skill reproduction, not only
current output.

## Algorithmic management changes the power relation

The workplace AI debate often centers on generative assistants.

But software can also become management infrastructure.

The OECD defines algorithmic management as software that fully or partly
automates tasks traditionally carried out by human managers. Its 2025 survey
covered more than 6,000 firms in France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the
United States.

The tools included work allocation, instructions, monitoring, target setting,
evaluation, rewards, and sanctions. Not all of these systems use AI, which is an
important boundary.

Managers often reported better decisions, but also concerns about unclear
accountability, logic they could not follow, and inadequate protection of worker
health.<SourceHook sourceId="algorithmic-management" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

When software saves managerial time by making control more scalable, workers may
receive no relief.

They may receive tighter measurement, lower discretion, and less room to explain
context.

This is another form of productivity: increased capacity to govern labor.

## Worker participation is not decorative

A small OECD laboratory study in three German manufacturing firms tested a
simulated algorithmic management system. Consultation among workers, managers,
and works council representatives produced designs participants considered able
to preserve productivity while improving job quality.

The study is limited. It does not prove that consultation always produces a fair
outcome. But it supports a practical point: worker participation can affect
technology design before harm is treated as inevitable.<SourceHook sourceId="worker-consultation" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

Workers need more than training on how to use a system.

They need power over whether and how it is introduced.

## Employment AI and the current legal boundary

The EU AI Act lists several employment and worker-management uses in Annex III
as high-risk. These include recruitment, selection, promotion, termination, task
allocation based on individual behavior or traits, and monitoring or evaluation
of performance.<SourceHook sourceId="employment-ai-classification" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

The current application timeline matters.

As of 8 August 2026, the AI Act is applying in stages. Article 50 transparency
requirements apply from 2 August 2026, and enforcement has begun for the rules
already applicable. Under the current EU implementation timeline, the
obligations for high-risk AI systems listed in Annex III apply from
2 December 2027 following the Digital Omnibus changes.<SourceHook sourceId="ai-act-timing" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

So the accurate claim is not that every workplace system already carries the
full Annex III compliance regime.

The accurate claim is:

- the law recognizes these uses as high-risk
- implementation remains staged
- other labor, data-protection, consultation, and anti-discrimination duties may
  already apply
- legal classification does not itself redistribute workplace power

Governance therefore needs:

- prior notice
- worker consultation
- meaningful explanation
- appeal
- limits on monitoring
- independent audit
- protection against retaliation
- technical support for unions and works councils
- procurement conditions tied to worker rights

## Uneven distribution

The same deployment lands differently depending on class, gender, migration
status, disability, age, care responsibilities, occupational segregation, and
employment security.

The ILO exposure index finds that women are more likely than men to be in the
highest-exposure occupations globally. The gap is larger in high-income
countries, partly because clerical work remains highly exposed.

This does not predict one uniform outcome.

It identifies a risk terrain shaped by existing labor markets.

Workers already expected to translate, coordinate, soften conflict, repair
processes, and support colleagues can also be pushed into hidden AI adoption
work.

That work must be counted and compensated.

## Macroeconomic restraint

Economy-wide forecasts remain uncertain.

Daron Acemoglu's 2024 paper models meaningful but comparatively modest
productivity effects over ten years. It is one model, not a final consensus, but
it provides a counterweight to claims that every organization will suddenly
become ten times more productive.<SourceHook sourceId="macroeconomic-caution" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

The honest position is:

- some tasks show substantial gains
- aggregate effects remain contested
- organizational costs are often hidden
- distribution determines social meaning

## The historical warning

Working-time limits were not automatic gifts from machinery.

The ILO's first convention in 1919 established an eight-hour day and
forty-eight-hour week for industrial undertakings. It reflected political
struggle and legal settlement, not the natural generosity of productivity
growth.<SourceHook sourceId="working-time-standard" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

A later ILO report found that more than one-third of workers globally regularly
worked more than forty-eight hours per week and argued for wider use of reduced
and flexible working-time arrangements.<SourceHook sourceId="working-time" sources={orientationSourceEntries} scope="orientation" />

The lesson is not that every AI gain should produce one identical policy.

The lesson is that time returns through institutions capable of distributing it.

## A non-surveillance time ledger

A **time ledger** is a Practice of Clarity proposal for tracking where
AI-related productivity gains go.

It is not an existing universal standard or legal duty.

Its purpose is not to measure every worker minute. Its purpose is to make the
redistribution of capacity visible at the level of a task, team, service, or
workflow.

A workable ledger begins with a bounded baseline:

1. Name the workflow that changed.
2. Record how time or effort was estimated before and after the change.
3. Record the new work created, including checking, correction, training,
   documentation, appeal, and repair.
4. Record what changed afterward in targets, staffing, pay, working hours,
   quality, autonomy, and risk.
5. Record who participated in the decision and who can challenge the result.

Where saved time cannot be measured reliably, record ranges, uncertainty, and
contested estimates rather than manufacture false precision.

It asks:

- what changed
- whose time was saved
- how the gain was estimated
- what new work appeared
- who performs verification
- whether targets, staffing, pay, or hours changed
- whether quality improved
- whether workers had a say
- whether affected people can appeal
- who received the financial benefit

The ledger must not track individual workers minute by minute.

Its unit should be the process and the distribution of capacity, not the worker
as a surveillance object.

Aggregated team data, sampled workflow studies, negotiated baselines, and
independent audits can all be used without turning the ledger into continuous
personal monitoring.

A time ledger can live in collective bargaining, works council consultation,
procurement, internal risk assessment, public-service reporting, or regulatory
documentation.

It needs anti-co-option rules:

- workers and their representatives help define the measures
- verification and repair work are included
- changes in targets and staffing remain visible
- the record is challengeable
- the ledger cannot become an individual performance score

Its function is simple:

> Count the gain. Make the transfer visible.

## What clarity asks

When AI is described as productive, ask:

- What became faster?
- What did not?
- Who measured the gain?
- Which costs were excluded?
- Who performs verification and repair?
- Did volume rise while quality fell?
- Did targets rise?
- Did staffing fall?
- Did learning pathways shrink?
- Did surveillance increase?
- Did workers gain time, pay, autonomy, or safety?
- Can workers inspect and challenge the system?
- Who owns the saved capacity?

These questions are not anti-technology.

They are anti-capture.

AI can create relief.

It can also scale existing ownership structures.

The difference is not hidden inside the model.

It is made through labor power, law, procurement, design, and public
accountability.
